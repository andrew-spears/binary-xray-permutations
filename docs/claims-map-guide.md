# Claims map: principles, data format, card-writing rules

The claims map is a graph of claims (nodes) joined by logical implications (edges). It is rendered from `graph.json`, which is generated by `build.py`. The human view is the dashboard `map/index.html` (§7 says how to open it); an earlier copy of the graph alone is the claude.ai artifact https://claude.ai/artifact/2pe8j5i5QgKJ5ouVYGJoRY.

Agents should read `graph.json` (machine-readable) and `state.md` (dense research state). Humans read the cards in the dashboard.

## 1. Every node is a logical statement

- **Exact statement.** Each node's `statement` is one precise mathematical claim: "for every …, there exists …" or "if …, then …". It may never be a description, a topic or a construction.
  - A construction becomes an if–then. Example: "if D splits into groups that each have a witness and whose block layout exists, then D has a witness."
  - A heuristic ("there's a pattern in X") becomes the strongest exact claim it suggests, or it goes in a card's text, not the graph.
- **Status refers to that exact statement.**
  - `proved`: proof sketch included.
  - `empirical`: holds in every test; the range is in `evidence`.
  - `open`: untested or partly tested.
  - `disproved`: a counterexample is known and is given.
  - `idea`: a proposed statement not yet tested.
- **Red means refuted, not "we didn't find it".** If a search for a rule or pattern fails, the open parent stays open. The specific rules that failed become red children, each a precise statement ("choosing the rightmost allowed diagonal always keeps the test passing") with its smallest counterexample.
- **No "a simple rule exists" nodes.** "Some deterministic procedure finds a witness" is trivially true if the conjecture is (try everything), so it's not a usable claim. "Simple" isn't defined, so it's not a mathematical claim either. Do this instead:
  - the parent is an existence statement that is weaker than the conjecture (see §2);
  - candidate rules are children;
  - a refinement like "the good step is recognized by a local condition" is allowed as its own precise node.
- **Don't refute something too strong and move on.** Before marking a rule red, check that its parent still states the weakest version that would suffice, so a subtler construction is still covered by an open node.

## 2. Induction routes use one template: invariant, start, step

Most proof routes are inductions. Write each one as an **invariant P on partial states**, with three claims:

1. **Start:** the initial state has P.
2. **Step:** every unfinished state with P has *some* next step that keeps P. The claim is existence, not a specific choice.
3. **Exact at the end:** a finished state with P is a real solution. This is often trivial.

In the graph:
- Each of these is its own node, joined by an ∧-junction into the goal. One clause in the edge's `why` says how the induction runs.
- Specific choices of step are red or open children of the step node.
- The invariant's card defines P and says **why it isn't the conjecture in disguise**: what makes P checkable or weaker than "has a witness".

Examples on the map:
- peeling: P = parity test, step = delete the top row;
- moves: P = has a witness, step = a minimal move from the start;
- decomposition: P = valid at smaller size, step = split into blocks.

## 3. Logic conventions

- An `implies`/`equiv` edge means the source(s) alone suffice. Joint premises use an array `from` (an ∧-junction). Several edges into one claim are alternative routes (OR), drawn merging into one arrow.
- **Case splits are explicit.** Split the cases into named sets joined by ∧ (e.g. 𝒩 = 𝒮 ∪ (𝒩∖𝒮); symmetric 𝒮 = odd ∪ even). Never draw "settles part of" edges. A family that covers part of a case is a `related` edge, or a sub-case in its own split.
- General results point to their special cases (inflation → direct sum → fixed point).
- `related` = informs without implying (thin grey line). Use it sparingly.
- No proved implication from proved premises into an unproved node.

## 4. Card-writing rules

1. **Exposition, then the claim.** The `intuition` text, shown first, is self-contained for someone who knows the four-views lab (stones / plank / board). In order:
   - (a) define the new notion in plain words;
   - (b) give an example with a picture;
   - (c) say how to recognise or use it;
   - (d) end with the claim in plain words that match `statement` exactly.

   No jargon without a plain definition.
2. **Examples belong to their card.** A picture must show what is special about *this* claim: a member of exactly the class the card talks about, the obstruction it rules out, or the before/after of its step. Don't reuse a neighbour's example unless it is genuinely the best illustration. Never use a case that another card already settles as the example of a remainder class.
3. **No numbers in the intuition** beyond the worked example. Ranges, counts, percentages and sources go in `evidence` and `refs`.
4. **Proved cards** carry a real proof sketch in `proof`, not `evidence`. Never mark something proved on computation alone.
5. **Statements claim exactly what is known.** Scope matches status, and limitations are written into the statement.
6. **Pictures:** every board with points is a valid witness of its D. Use before/after pairs for operations. With no natural picture, give a fully worked small example in `example`.
7. **Empirical cards** say how the check was done, and whether it depends on the conjecture at the tested sizes.
8. **Idea and open cards get the most care**, since they are what Andrew reads most: an elegant explanation, a worked example, what the claim would prove, and what has been tried.
9. **Information hygiene:** a card describes only its own claim. It doesn't summarise the cards that imply it. Why a card implies its parent belongs in the edge's `why`.
10. **Red cards stay short:** statement, one sentence, smallest counterexample.

## 5. Data format

- `areas[]` {id, label}: grouping (families, moves, peeling, algebra, ideas), used to order siblings.
- `nodes[]` {id, area ("goal" for conj, notight, tightcut), title, status ∈ proved|empirical|open|disproved|idea, intuition, pics?, statement, proof?, evidence?, example?, refs?, vis? {file, title}, active?}. Added by the build: `route` (id of its proof route), `added` and `since` (when the card appeared and when it got its present status, UTC), `was?` (the status before).
- `routes[]` {id, name, state ∈ active|stalled|open, summary, gap, key}, `highlights[]` {kind ∈ lead|result|dead, id, text}, `families[]` {id, group, name, status, card, parent, test, example, text, check?}: the dashboard's curated text, from `map/dashboard_data.py` (§7).
- `edges[]` {from (id, or array = ∧-junction), to, kind ∈ implies|equiv|idea|related, status ∈ proved|unchecked, why}.
- Picture specs:
  - board {t:"board", rows, cols (missing = holes), D, pts [[r,c]], w [[r,c,"½"]], cand [[r,c]] (ringed: options), blocks [[r0,r1,c0,c1]], par: true, cap};
  - stones {t:"stones", lo, hi, D, hl, cap};
  - pair {t:"pair", a, b, mid, cap}.

## 6. Layout (for reference)

- Rows = longest path to the conjecture.
- Each node hangs under one parent; subtrees are contour-packed.
- Arrows into one claim merge into one orthogonal bus.
- Each card shows its route to the conjecture as a breadcrumb.

## 7. The dashboard: data, opening, publishing

**What the build needs from you.** A new card must be listed in exactly one route in `map/dashboard_data.py` (`ROUTES[...]["nodes"]`); `build.py` stops with the missing ids otherwise. Nothing else is required: dates are kept by the build in `map/card_dates.txt`, and the "Latest changes" list comes from them. If `card_dates.txt`, `graph.json` or `graph.js` has a merge conflict, rerun `python3 map/build.py` and commit the result.

**Curated text** (`map/dashboard_data.py`). Route summaries, the "what is missing" line of a route, the highlights on the front page and the family descriptions are written by hand. The rules of §4 apply to them: every sentence must be checkable against the card it points to; "tested" for what is tested; a complete computer search is not a proof; and say who checked a proof when no person has read it. A family's `example` must be a valid arrangement of that family (the build checks validity).

**Families.** The membership tests are `T1` and `FAM_TESTS` in `map/index.html`; the same tests are in `map/labs/covered.html`. Change both together when a new family is proved.

**Opening it.**
- From disk: open `map/index.html` in a browser. The pages read `graph.js`, so no server is needed.
- As one file: `python3 map/bundle.py` writes `map/dashboard-single.html` (not tracked by git), with the data, the labs and the graph inlined. It opens from a message attachment in any browser.
- As a link, with GitHub Pages. Pages is **not** switched on, and switching it on makes the published pages readable by anyone who has the address, even though the repository is private. Pages for a private repository also needs a paid GitHub plan (Pro or Team); on the free plan the repository would have to be public. Two ways:
  1. *Only the `map/` folder (recommended).* Settings → Pages → Source: **GitHub Actions**. Then Settings → Secrets and variables → Actions → Variables → New repository variable `PUBLISH_MAP` with value `true`. Then Actions → **Publish map** → Run workflow. After that, every push to `main` that touches `map/` republishes. The workflow is `.github/workflows/publish-map.yml`.
  2. *The whole repository.* Settings → Pages → Source: **Deploy from a branch**, branch `main`, folder `/ (root)`. The root `index.html` forwards to `map/`. This also publishes `docs/` and `agents/`.

  Either way the address is https://andrew-spears.github.io/binary-xray-permutations/.

**Checking a change to the pages.** Headless Chrome is enough: `google-chrome --headless --no-sandbox --disable-gpu --window-size=390,844 --virtual-time-budget=8000 --screenshot=out.png "file://$PWD/map/index.html#/families"`. Addresses: `#/overview`, `#/families`, `#/routes`, `#/routes/<route id>`, `#/cards`, `#/graph/<card id>`; add `~<card id>` or `~f.<family id>` to open a card or a family.
